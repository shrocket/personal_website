I was lucky enough, after reading "Thoughts on the Esterházy Court Uniform," to find an essay by Robin Purves entitled [_A Commentary on J.H. Prynne's "Thoughts on the Esterházy Court Uniform."_](https://knowledge.lancashire.ac.uk/id/eprint/3992/2/Purves%2C%20Article%2C%20Commentary%20on%20Prynne.pdf)

In the essay, Purves says that the now twice-mentioned poem "was the first poem by J.H. Prynne that I frequently reread."

I have only read each of the poems in this book once. Reading this book was my first exposure to Prynne. I feel that I am in no way qualified to comment on Prynne or his poems.

Thankfully, at the beginning of his essay, Purves also wrote

> Comprehension of the text is a more arduous process to undergo than administering it to yourself as a kind of homoeopathic remedy and attaining the state where you are able to explain what you think you understand of the poem is more difficult yet. . . .

I have, as I said, only administered this book to myself once. "Thoughts on the Esterházy Court Uniform" wasn't even one of the poems I think I would like to administer to myself again. That being said, I cannot think of a reason not to: Nearly every single poem in this book made me feel as though, perhaps, if we could just find a way, as a culture, as a species to--

But maybe I'm just trying to restate (hyperbolically) something Charles Olson said better: "I keep thinking, it comes to this: culture displacing the state."

From "In the Long Run, to be Stranded"

```
        The stirring is so
    slight, the talk so stunned, the
city warm in the air, it is a
too steady shift and life as
it's called is age and the merest impulse,
    called the city and the deep
    blunting damage of hope.

```
