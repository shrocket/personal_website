I noticed the single, black hair on his nose the moment we sat down to coffee. We talked about words, and sentences, and paragraphs. I couldn't stop looking at the hair. It wasn't long enough simply to reach out and remove, but it was too long to ignore.

What happened in the red, '92 Toyota Corolla hatchback with a spoiler, black leather interior, green hood, and a dent in the right-front quarter panel shaped exactly like Ronald Reagan's face? What happened?

Is the wind up here on the hill worse than it might be down in the valley?

Eventually, he ran his hand across his face, and moved the hair from his nose to his lower lip, where it sat and wobbled slightly as he spoke.

The biggest problem American minds have with the metric system, especially in terms of length, is this: Americans think in inches, feet, and miles; Europeans think in centimeters, meters, and kilometers. Inches are longer than centimeters. Feet are _shorter_ than meters. Miles are, again, longer than kilometers. The relationship is therefore twisted, and difficult to reconcile. Distances expressed in meters always seem ridiculously short.

A word starting with the letter P blew the hair from his lip and into his coffee, where it floated while he continued to talk about the length of German sentences.

The tallest mountain in North America is 20,320 feet tall. The tallest mountain in Europe is _only_ 5,642 meters tall.

After he finished his sentence, he looked down, made a face of vague disgust, picked the hair out of the coffee, and flicked it onto the floor.

It is raining, and the sun is shining.

The hair's journey was approximately 1.75 meters, and took half an hour.

Despite its owner's fervent prayers, nothing ever happened in the Toyota. One time, a passenger exited the Toyota is it was traveling at approximately 93 miles per hour, but most of what happened then happened outside the Toyota, not in it.

I just watched a helicopter seed propeller upwards.

93 miles per hour is 150 kilometers per hour, which seems much faster.
